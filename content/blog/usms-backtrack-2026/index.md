Less than a year after instrumenting their categorical ban on trans women competing, US Masters Swimming (USMS) is deciding to comply with state laws. This is a win for women in sports, and the first reversal we are seeing in the Trump era of transmisogyny. First I'll go over the timeline, then my own experiences as a transgender swimmer in the past year, and then I'll briefly speculate on the implications of what I believe democratic politicians should take away from this news.

## The Timeline

USMS has a monopoly on adult swim competition in the United States. There are no other notable options for swimmers to get together and race. Also notably, because USMS provides swim teams with insurance to cover the risk of injury in an aquatic setting, it solves for a niche that is a barrier to organized swim practices outside of youth recreational swim or competition within the school system. Effectively, it is the only game in town.

In 2005, USMS adopted its [first transgender inclusion policy](https://www.usms.org/volunteer-central/policy-and-governance/usms-policies/transgender-swimmer-inclusion). In it, they allowed transmasculine swimmers to swim in the Men's category without any special conditions, and they allowed trans women to swim as long as they had been on hormones for a year, and could prove that their testosterone levels were below 5nmol/L consistently over the past year. Trans women were not required to provide that evidence up front, but if someone challenged them on their trans status, they would be required to produce that evidence, and the league encouraged trans women to submit that information upfront when registering. This policy stood for twenty years. It had some faults for sure, and reasonable minds can discuss the burden of testing, the transmedicalism / eurocentrism of setting their bar at 5nmol of testosterone, and so on. But the policy was uncontroversial for two decades and trans people were able to integrate into the adult swimming community. I don't think there have ever been a lot of us, but we were out there.

Then, in 2025, 6 months into the Trump regime, a transsexual woman named Ana Caldas did the unconscionable – she won a few events. [Caldas won first at nationals](https://www.swimmingworldmagazine.com/news/us-masters-swimming-under-investigation-after-alleged-trans-athlete-wins-five-gold-at-nationals/) in the 50 and 100 yard breaststroke, the 50 and 100 yard freestyle, and the 100 yard individual medley relays, in the age 45-49 age bracket. She's also jacked as hell, competes in CrossFit, and can step on me.

After a [Texas AG investigation into USMS](https://www.lgbtqnation.com/2025/05/texas-ag-investigates-us-masters-swimming-after-trans-woman-wins-5-first-place-finishes/), the organization immediately folded, and announced their [new transgender "inclusion" policy](https://www.usms.org/volunteer-central/policy-and-governance/usms-policies/interim-eligibility-policy). Trans women were allowed to attend swim meets, but they were not eligible to be included in "Recognition Programs". There was no degree of medical transition that would be considered. Recognition programs include things like national records, a "top 10 swimmers" list that they apparently maintain, and notably results of swim meets. 

I was in communications with USMS on July 2, 2025, asking clarifying questions about how eligibility would work under their new system. I was about 6 months into reaching their qualifying hormonal levels, and had been anticipating my own eligibility in 2026. The text of the original ban read:

> Results for members who seek to swim in a category that does not align with their sex assigned at birth and who are not otherwise eligible for Recognition Programs will be removed from Recognition Programs. 

So I asked them if I would be able to compete under this category without the hormone levels, and if so:
* Would I be able to score points for my team?
* Would any relay I was in be disqualified from scoring points for my team?
* What does this mean for states that have laws protecting trans people?
* Would my times from meets still be tracked by the league?

I heard back from a representative for the league, who told me

> "We are still working through the display of results in a member’s record and will provide an update as soon as practical. This policy does not give a local meet hosts the leeway to work outside the national policy or be a localized decision. The policy applies to all sanctioned events across the country.
> 
> You will not be eligible to score points for your team. 
> You would not be able swim on relays without the relay being removed from results as well, the relay would not be scored."

Sometime between then and now, they revised the 2025 policy to read:

> Results for members who seek to swim in a category that does not align with their sex assigned at birth and who are not otherwise eligible for Recognition Programs will be removed from Recognition Programs. **Those results will remain in the results database.**

So, they did at least make a decision about throwing out our results, not that they ever got back to me about it personally. They also informed me that it would be my responsibility to approach every meet organizer that I participated in that I was trans, and that they would have to manually exclude me from the results of their meet.

I told them to kick rocks.

In January of 2026 I decided to re-register as a woman, and this time there was new scary language added to their website. At least twice in the registration process, as a woman, I was given a prompt to attest that I was certified female at birth. They told me of the consequences of getting witch hunted, and how I would be kicked out if I refused chromosomal testing. (Which Ana reportedly did refuse to provide to  to World Aquatics, and [has been banned over](https://www.swimmingworldmagazine.com/news/swimmer-banned-for-five-years-for-refusing-gender-verification-test-after-world-aquatics-investigation/). WA is even worse, and I'm happy that USMS is cleaving less to their shit policies). I elected to not press the "oppress me" button, and have been participating in competitions again without incident.

### The Walk Back

Yesterday, on March 12, [USMS sent out an email](https://swimswam.com/u-s-masters-swimming-updates-interim-eligibility-policy-adds-mens-open-category/) that they were going to start complying with state and local laws. The policy now broadly says "members will be eligible based on gender identity and/or gender expression in applicable jurisdictions." They walked back the draconian testing policies in favor of "we may ask to see your government-issued ID", and they kept the ban on national level competition in place.

It is a qualified win, but one that is meaningful for someone in my situation. I have no national aspirations, but I want to participate in the events that my teammates go to. Swim practices, happy hours, holiday parties, and of course swim meets. That is my right under California law, and if we had a democratic governor, my rights under those laws might have actually been defended. But now it's clear - I have the right to compete exactly as I have been, and the new policy makes it clear that I don't have to defend myself if I'm accused of being trans in the moment, even at a national level. This is a more respectful and safer environment for all cisgender, transgender, and intersex women.

The situation still definitely sucks for nonbinary people though. Let's not pretend renaming the Men's category the Men's/Open category is actually an adequate response for the wide range of gender diversity in the trans community.

It's unclear what exactly led to their backtrack. Probably a substantial part of it was the clear illegality under multiple state laws. But I know from talking with other women that they were made uncomfortable by the new registration process and how "in your face" it was. I was also told by another swimmer that a cis woman decided to mark herself as trans to see what would happen. She got pulled into a meeting with executives at USMS where they had a conversation with her about it. The fact that they would have done the same to me feels somewhat chilling.

I know that cis allies and many teams wrote to the league about these policies. Even though the legal risk may be the driving factor, I have plenty of reason to believe that the league was making hasty choices they weren't happy with, and the pushback from everyday swimmers did matter in this choice.

## What this means to me

It's hard to capture how important swimming has been to me in words. I grew up swimming from age 8 to 10 or so. I swam all the way through elementary, middle, and high school, and I competed in my freshman year in college. It's been a place where I both had a wonderful freedom in a coed sport, as well as feelings of dysmorphia that I wouldn't understand for decades the experience of seeing other girls with their chests covered, and realizing that I was required to go topless is such a formative experience for me finally gathering the confidence to wear a one piece and begin competing in one was an important step along my journey of understanding who I am as a woman. If you are my friend or a lover, you know how important swimming is in my life.

When I was still getting my hormone levels to balance, it was frustrating. I was making medical choices about the amount of hormones that I was taking, the decision to take androgen blockers, and how often I wanted to get blood work done, around the question of my eligibility to compete. That had an impact on my life, and my health. I was really only able to process how much I was investing into that hope for the future in July 2025, when that hope was stripped away. Getting banned had a significant impact on my mental health. It is not an exaggeration to say that every time that I put on my suit and got in the pool for practice, that I was thinking about the fact that the league didn't want me there. 

At this point in my life, aside from the general onslaught of news and the horrors, there have really only been two direct ways that anti-trans legislation has reached me as a Californian. I no longer have the ability to renew my passport without having my gender marker revoked and, I was banned from competing as a woman unconditionally in my sport. I thought a lot about the unfairness of this. I read about the histories of competing in women's sports, and the history of the debate around trans athletes. I recommend the work of [Katie Barnes](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250276636/fairplay/) and [Rose Eveleth](https://www.npr.org/people/1255807297/rose-eveleth) on the topic. All this to say, while I'm not on the forefront of trans oppression as a white Californian, the USMS policy has had a huge impact on me personally.

It's also a very isolating experience. I'm aware that there are other trans swimmers in the country who were affected by the policy, but I've never met a single other trans woman in the league. I could talk to my teammates and my friends about this, and how it was affecting me, but there was no support really available besides commiseration. I didn't think that there was going to be an end in sight. I didn't think that Gavin Newsom was ever going to take a stand on my behalf, because he hates trans women. He has repeatedly pointed to trans athletes as a minority he wants to throw under the bus, and I had every reason to believe he would never enforce California laws.

So I felt alone, and especially alone when I decided to defy the organization, and register as a "stealth" athlete. I felt the need to self-censor on my own website. That, if I had too much of a profile, I was going to get banned from my sport. This community has meant a lot to me in the past few years. It's a thing that I can do on a regular basis, a way to push myself, get stronger as an adult, and feel sound of body and mind alongside peers who I've really come to enjoy. Losing that would be devastating. At the last meet, I went to my team had four women for the first time in essentially the history of our team. It's an LGBT team so historically most of the members have been gay men. Because I was there, we had enough women to have a relay race. The excitement and energy of my teammates to race alongside me and support each other at that meet meant the world to me. It was the very first women's relay that I've ever been able to swim in. That's what gets lost in the "debate". The camaraderie. The joy.

## Political Implications

There's a sense in which this policy change doesn't make a substantial difference for many people. Trans women are still banned at the national level. Many, many women live in states with increasingly awful anti-trans laws. There are battles that need to be fought for as long of a political future as I can imagine. However, this is the first time I have personally seen anti-trans, anti-women's sports policies get walked back in the second Trump administration. And I think that is noteworthy.

Political operatives like Gavin Newsom believe that there is substantial upside to throwing trans sports and gender-affirming care for children under the bus in service of their political ambition. They look at polling, which superficially shows that the intuition of the average American is that we should be kept out of sports. I think this is shortsighted, for two reasons.

The first reason is that eventually, the whole population will come to be impacted by these methods for policing gender. And they will hate it. They will know who decided to institute it, and they will resent them for it. Cis women were made to check a box while registering to sign up, and suddenly the hypothetical of what it meant for "should trans women be allowed" turned into a reality of this could be wielded against you". Most people don't think about that possibility until it's in their face, but when they do, it will be unpopular, and the politicians responsible will be tied to their choices there. We've seen how many politicians running on anti-trans policies have failed in the last couple of years, often in significant upsets. This is in no small part because of the second reason:

A poll question doesn't measure how deeply someone cares about something. Someone who has never knowingly met a trans person can think about "should they kick out the trannies" as a sick sort of intellectual exercise. It's not a real question the same way their wages or grocery prices are. These polls are never conducted in a way that they would ask questions like "should women athletes be subject to genital inspections " or "should we mandate chromosomal testing for amateur athletes." Instead they ask "should we allow men in little girls sports" - a wildly disingenuous question because it stealthily elides both gender and age to construct a mental image that has no basis in the reality of the situation. 

I refuse to engage with the science or justification in this moment, but all available data points to transsexual women having [no detectable competitive advantage](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33288617/) over cis women after >=1 years on HRT. Beyond that, the number of trans women who feel inclined to compete in sports in the first place are vanishingly small, and there's a serious conversation to be had about how important competitive "fairness" is, relative to the benefits of social cohesion that come from acceptance and inclusion. But I digress.

My point is this – banning trans women from sports is not some holy grail opinion that is going to bring moderate candidates victory through some kind of middle ground strategy. We can point to USMS as a data point that these policies can be resisted, through local fights and legislative victories towards inclusion. Even now, in the peak of Trump's power as he drags us into war with Iran, a world where trans women are banned from competing is not inevitable. Candidates who have the conviction and the courage to stand up for us like Zohran Mamdani are the only viable future for the party, and leaders need to remember that they have the power to shape public opinion, not just to pander to it.
