> "I don't hate you. I sympathize with you"

It had been maybe 10 emails from Ms. Friday's anti-trans mailing list before I replied to one. "Why do you hate us so much?" She was organizing a rally to criminilize trans women using the restroom in California, up in Sacramento. I'd looked her up, learned that her radicalization point was when her child came out as trans to her at age 13. But to upend her entire life to pursue the cause persecuting trans people, it still felt impossible. Like a comic-book villain, with two-dimensional motivations. In my experience, people aren't like this. Even people with extremely anti-trans beliefs know how to act like a person in the real world. Terfism is a thing of the internet, of activists putting on a performance for politicians. Or in particularly hateful cases, to try to provoke violence against trans people at their place of work, or in a restroom. But in a face-to-face conversation, I had a hard time envisioning someone being able to resist every human impulse to reciprocate kindness when it's extended to them. So when Ms. Friday responded "would you like to have a conversation?", I was interested. Was there a trace of curiosity about my experience? Could an experience of a trans person free from the artifice of political theater possibly temper her harshest impulses? This is the train of that that landed me a coffee shop on a Friday afternoon, with a woman would gleefully destroy me and my loved ones, hiding behind a fake smile and bottomless condescension.

Leading up to the conversation, I was thinking ahead. What would she ask me? Would I represent myself well? Or my community? What if she turns on me specifically? Someone with her kind of reach in anti-trans circles could be a real danger to me. My loved ones made sure I had plans to share my location, and to confirm my safety after talking with her. After the conversation, I'm a little less worried about that kind of danger. She doesn't think she's hateful. This isn't the righteous anger of a religious zealot. She doesn't think of me as a person who could be the object of hatred. Just a thing to be used or discarded. So I think I can speak my mind without fear of retribution.

---

As I brought my coffee over and sat down, she thanked me for meeting with her. Before I had really said much of anything, she told me "I don't hate you. I sympathize with you." To her credit, she asked me about my story, and what I knew of her. After that back and forth, I asked her why she wanted to talk with me.
